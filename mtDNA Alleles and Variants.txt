Across the global human population, there is no fixed single number of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) alleles—instead, researchers track tens of thousands of documented variants across the **16,569 base pairs** and **37 genes** of the human mitochondrial genome.

Because mtDNA does not undergo sexual recombination and mutates rapidly, population-level variation is cataloged at several different scales:

* **Documented Single-Nucleotide Variants (SNVs) & Indels:** Large reference databases like **MITOMAP** (which aggregates over 66,800 full-length human mtDNA sequences from GenBank) track roughly **20,000 to 24,000 distinct reported variants** across both coding and non-coding (control) regions. Population cohorts like **gnomAD** independently catalog over **10,800 unique variants** occurring at more than half of all base positions in the mitochondrial genome.
* **Per-Site Allelic Diversity:** At any single nucleotide position, there can theoretically be up to **4 alleles** (A, C, G, or T, plus occasional insertions/deletions). In practice, most positions are biallelic (the reference base plus one common transition, such as A→G or C→T), though hypervariable segments in the non-coding D-loop (Control Region) frequently exhibit triallelic and quadrallelic sites across human populations.
* **Pathogenic vs. Benign Alleles:** The vast majority of these ~20,000+ alleles are neutral polymorphisms that define maternal ancestry. Only around **1,000 variants** have been reported in association with disease, with roughly **100 confirmed pathogenic mutations** (such as `m.3243A>G` in *MT-TL1* for MELAS or `m.11778G>A` in *MT-ND4* for Leber hereditary optic neuropathy).
* **Haplotypes & Haplogroups (Whole-Molecule "Alleles"):** Because the entire circular mtDNA molecule is inherited intact as a single non-recombining linkage block, population geneticists often treat specific combinations of linked alleles as **haplotypes**. Tens of thousands of unique full-sequence haplotypes exist globally, which are grouped into **~30 major maternal haplogroups** (e.g., L0–L6, M, N, H, U, J, T) and more than **5,000 named sub-haplogroups** on the human mtDNA phylogenetic tree (PhyloTree).

Compared to mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), human **nuclear DNA** is roughly **180,000 times larger** and harbors orders of magnitude more genetic variation across the human population—even though its mutation rate per base pair is dramatically lower.

| --- | --- | --- |
| **Genome Size (Haploid)** | **16,569 base pairs** (1 circular chromosome) | **~3.1 billion base pairs** (23 linear chromosomes; ~6.2B diploid) |
| **Protein-Coding Genes** | **13 genes** (plus 22 tRNAs and 2 rRNAs) | **~19,800–20,000 genes** (plus ~25,000+ non-coding RNA genes) |
| **Copies per Somatic Cell** | **~100 to 10,000+ copies** (polyploid/heteroplasmic) | **2 copies** (diploid: 1 maternal, 1 paternal) |
| **Cataloged SNVs / Indels** | **~20,000–24,000** (MITOMAP) | **>1.1 billion** validated variants (dbSNP / gnomAD) |
| **Variant Density** | **~1+ variant per base pair** (in global cohorts) | **~1 variant per 3 base pairs** (in global cohorts) |
| **Variants in One Person** | **~10 to 80** differences from reference (rCRS) | **~4 to 5 million** differences from reference (GRCh38) |
| **Mutation Rate (per bp/gen)** | **~10–20× faster** (~$10^{-7}$ to $10^{-6}$) | **Baseline** (~$1.2 \times 10^{-8}$) |

### Key Structural Differences in "Allele" Counts

1. **Raw Variant Volume vs. Density:** Because the nuclear genome spans 3.1 billion base pairs, databases like **dbSNP** and **gnomAD** have cataloged over **1.1 billion distinct single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels** across human populations—dwarfing the ~24,000 known mtDNA variants. However, on a *per-base-pair* basis, mtDNA is actually more saturated with observed variants because of its high mutation rate and lack of histones and robust mismatch repair.
2. **Gene-Level Allelic Diversity:** In nuclear DNA, alleles are typically defined at the **gene or locus level** rather than the whole-chromosome level. While most nuclear genes have dozens to thousands of rare coding variants in global databases (like *BRCA1* or *CFTR*), hyper-polymorphic immune loci such as the **HLA (MHC) genes** on Chromosome 6 have over **38,000 distinct named alleles** cataloged in the IPD-IMGT/HLA database for that single gene complex alone—exceeding the entire variant catalog of the mitochondrial genome.
3. **Shuffling vs. Linked Blocks:** Nuclear alleles constantly recombine during meiosis, creating virtually infinite combinations of alleles across chromosomes. By contrast, because mtDNA never recombines, all ~10–80 variants an individual carries remain permanently locked together on the same circular molecule, forming discrete maternal lineages (haplogroups) rather than independent Mendelian alleles.
Compared to humans, the common fruit fly (**Drosophila melanogaster**) has a much more compact nuclear genome—roughly **22 times smaller**—while its mitochondrial genome is slightly **larger** due to an expanded non-coding control region.

Because *Drosophila* has a massive effective population size and rapid generation time (~10 days), its per-site genetic diversity is actually **higher** than that of humans, even though its total raw variant count is smaller.

| --- | --- | --- |
| **Genome Size (Haploid)** | **~140–180 million bp** (4 chromosome pairs: X/Y, 2, 3, 4) | **~19,517–19,524 base pairs** (1 circular chromosome) |
| **Protein-Coding Genes** | **~13,900–14,000 genes** | **13 genes** (plus 22 tRNAs and 2 rRNAs — same core set as humans) |
| **Copies per Somatic Cell** | **2 copies** (diploid; note: larval salivary glands are polytene with ~1,000+ copies) | **~100 to 1,000+ copies** (maternally inherited) |
| **Cataloged SNVs / Indels** | **~5 to 6 million** natural variants (DGRP / *Drosophila* Evolution over Space and Time) | **~1,500 to 2,500** documented natural variants across wild lines |
| **Nucleotide Diversity ($\pi$)** | **~0.5% to 1.0%** (roughly **5–10× higher** diversity per bp than humans) | **~0.1% to 0.4%** (varies sharply due to *Wolbachia* selective sweeps) |
| **Mutation Rate (per bp/gen)** | **~$2.8 \times 10^{-9}$** (lower per generation, but ~35 generations/year) | **~$6.2 \times 10^{-8}$** (~20× higher than fly nuclear DNA) |
| **Recombination** | **Females only** (male *Drosophila* have zero meiotic recombination) | **None** (maternally inherited block) |

### Key Quirks of Fruit Fly DNA Counts

1. **Larger mtDNA Than Humans (The A+T-Rich Region):** While human mtDNA is 16,569 bp, *D. melanogaster* mtDNA is **~19,520 bp**. The extra ~3,000 bp isn't extra genes—it carries the exact same 37 genes as human mtDNA—but an unusually long, repetitive **A+T-rich non-coding control region** (~4.6 kb, composed of >90% adenine and thymine).
2. **Higher Per-Base Allelic Diversity:** Because wild *Drosophila* populations number in the billions, natural selection and drift maintain far more alleles per base pair than in humans. Any two wild-caught fruit flies differ at roughly **1 in every 100 to 200 nuclear base pairs** (~700,000 to 1.4 million SNPs between two individual flies), compared to ~1 in 1,000 base pairs between two humans.
3. **Lab Strains vs. Wild Alleles (FlyBase):** In addition to natural population alleles, geneticists have engineered and cataloged over **200,000+ induced mutant alleles, transgenes, and chromosomal inversions (balancers)** in **FlyBase**, making *Drosophila* one of the most heavily annotated allelic libraries in biology.
4. **The *Wolbachia* Hitchhiking Effect:** *Drosophila* mtDNA diversity is heavily shaped by **Wolbachia**, an intracellular bacterium passed through egg cytoplasm alongside mitochondria. When an advantageous *Wolbachia* strain sweeps through a fly population, it drags a single linked mtDNA haplotype along with it, periodically wiping out mitochondrial allelic diversity across entire continents.

Across eukaryotes, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) varies wildly in size—from **under 6,000 base pairs** in malaria parasites to **over 11 million base pairs** in flowering plants—even though almost all species use it to build the exact same core electron-transport machinery.

While **animals (bilaterians)** locked into a hyper-compact, nearly identical ~16–17 kb blueprint of **37 genes** roughly 500 million years ago, **plants, fungi, and protists** went in completely different evolutionary directions, bloating their mtDNA with introns, repetitive junk DNA, and horizontal gene transfers—or fragmenting it into multiple tiny mini-circles.

### Three Macro-Evolutionary Patterns

1. **The Animal "Frozen Accident" (~16 kb / 37 Genes):** Almost every vertebrate—from a mouse (16,299 bp) to a blue whale (16,402 bp) to a human (16,569 bp)—carries the exact same 37 genes in the exact same order. Selection ruthlessly purged non-coding DNA in animals to speed up replication in metabolically active cells.
2. **The Plant Size Paradox (Large Genome, Low Mutation Rate):** Flowering plants have enormous, recombining mtDNA molecules (often split into multiple sub-genomic circles) that absorb foreign DNA from the nucleus and chloroplasts. Ironically, while plant mtDNA gains and loses structural chunks rapidly, its **point-mutation rate is ~100× slower** than animal mtDNA.
3. **Fragmented "Mini-Circle" Exceptions:** Not all mtDNA is a single chromosome. **Human body lice** (*Pediculus humanus*) shattered their 37 genes across **20 separate circular mini-chromosomes** (3–4 kb each), while the **cucumber** (*Cucumis sativus*, ~1.68 Mb) and **silene** plants (*Silene conica*, ~11.3 Mb) spread their mitochondrial genes across dozens of autonomous circular chromosomes.

Fungal and mushroom mitochondrial genomes sit right in the middle of the evolutionary spectrum—larger and far more structurally chaotic than animal mtDNA, yet much smaller than flowering plant mtDNA.

Across Kingdom Fungi, mtDNA sizes span from **~11,000 bp** in simple yeasts up to **>530,000 bp** in certain mushrooms, with a median size around **58,000 bp**. Despite this massive 50-fold swing in length, almost all fungi rely on the exact same **14 to 15 core protein-coding genes**.

### Four Defining Quirks of Mushroom & Fungal mtDNA

1. **The "Intron Invasion" (Same Genes, Wildly Different Sizes):**
Mushrooms like the white button (*Agaricus bisporus*, 135 kb) and the thick-footed morel (*Morchella crassipes*, 531 kb) carry virtually the exact same core respiratory genes as a tiny 11 kb yeast. The difference is **mobile self-splicing introns (Group I and Group II)** that carry their own "homing endonuclease" genes. These selfish genetic elements copy-paste themselves inside mitochondrial genes like `cox1` and `cob`, inflating the genome by hundreds of thousands of base pairs without adding any metabolic function.
2. **The 15th Core Gene (`rps3` & `atp9`):**
While animals have 13 mitochondrial protein-coding genes, mushrooms typically have **15**: they retain **`atp9`** (a subunit of ATP synthase that animals moved to the nucleus) and **`rps3`** (a ribosomal protein gene embedded inside an intron of the large rRNA gene). Conversely, many yeasts (*S. cerevisiae*, *S. pombe*) lost all 7 Complex I (`nad`) genes, leaving them with just **8** core proteins.
3. **Linear vs. Circular Chromosomes (Even Within the Same Species):**
Unlike animals—where circular mtDNA is the universal rule—many yeasts and mushrooms possess **linear mtDNA** capped with specialized telomeric loops (`t-loops`) and terminal inverted repeats. Remarkably, in species like *Candida parapsilosis*, some strains have circular mtDNA while other strains of the exact same species have linear mtDNA.
4. **Biparental & "Doubly Uniparental" Inheritance:**
Unlike humans (strictly maternal), mushrooms don't have simple male/female sexes—they have thousands of mating types. When two compatible mushroom mycelia fuse (plasmogamy), their nuclei share cytoplasm, meaning **both parents can contribute mitochondria** to the resulting fruiting body, frequently leading to high rates of mitochondrial recombination and heteroplasmy in wild mushrooms.

This is a viable bioengineering framework—essentially **comparative mitonuclear synthetic biology**. Nature has already run billions of years of parallel stress-testing on mitochondrial genomes across extreme environments, and we can systematically mine those adaptations.

However, one biological reality dictates how this catalog must be built: **mitochondrial genes almost never work as plug-and-play parts across species.** Because the 13 animal mtDNA proteins must physically lock into ~70+ nuclear-encoded subunits to form the electron transport chain (OXPHOS), dropping a raw gene from one species into another usually causes structural mismatch, electron leakage, and toxic reactive oxygen species (ROS).

To make cross-species mitochondrial adaptations transferable—and find combinations greater than the sum of their parts—the catalog has to track **functional mechanisms and thermodynamic trade-offs**, not just raw sequences.
